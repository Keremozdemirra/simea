# Verified bibliography — paper 02

**Verification pass completed 12 August 2026.**

Every reference in [POSITIONING.md](POSITIONING.md) was checked against a
publisher record, DOI landing page, or the publisher's own catalogue entry.
**Nothing was removed — every work exists as cited.** Ten corrections were
needed, touching twelve of the twenty entries; those are listed in §2.

**What "verified" means here, precisely.** Author, title, year, venue, volume,
issue and page range were confirmed against publisher or DOI-level records, and
against RePEc/EconPapers where the publisher page was paywalled. It does **not**
mean the page images were inspected, and it does not verify that any work says
what the positioning note claims it says. That is a separate reading pass and it
has not been done.

## 2. Corrections made

Twelve of the twenty entries were wrong or incomplete as they stood in the
positioning note. None was wrong in a way that changes the argument. Four —
rows 1 to 4 below — would have been outright errors in a submitted manuscript:
a source cited in the body and absent from the bibliography, a chapter dated to
its preprint, two separately published parts merged into one citation, and a
thirteen-year priority obscured.

| # | Reference | What was wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Soros (1987) | **Cited in §3.3 of the note but missing from the reading list entirely.** The most consequential omission found. |
| 2 | Aaronson | Dated 2013, which is the arXiv preprint. The published version is 2016, in *The Once and Future Turing*, ch. 12, pp. 193–296. Citing a preprint year against a book chapter is the kind of thing a copy-editor catches and an author should not need them to. |
| 3 | Popper (1950) | Listed as "1(2–3)", which compresses two separately published parts into one citation. Parts I and II have distinct issue and page ranges and should be cited separately. |
| 4 | Popper (1957) | The Oedipus effect argument first appeared in *Economica* in 1944–45; the 1957 book is the collected edition. Citing only 1957 obscures a thirteen-year priority. |
| 5 | Lo (2004) | Short title only. The full title carries the subtitle "market efficiency from an evolutionary perspective", which matters here because §5's objection is specifically about the *evolutionary* framing. |
| 6 | Arthur et al. (1997) | "Arthur, W. B. et al." with no venue. Five authors, and it is a book chapter, not an article. |
| 7 | Lucas (1976) | No venue. It is a Carnegie-Rochester conference volume, not a journal article. |
| 8 | Samuelson (1965) | No issue or pages. |
| 9 | Laplace (1814) | No publisher; no translation identified, which a reader chasing the demon passage in English will need. |
| 10 | Wolfram, Zuboff, Thaler & Sunstein | Publisher and place absent throughout. |

## 3. Findings beyond the citations themselves

**Hayek (1945) is in the reading list and is never cited in the body.** Nor are
Simon (1955), Kahneman & Tversky (1979), Thaler & Sunstein (2008), Zuboff (2019)
or Arthur et al. (1997). That is legitimate for a reading list, but five of the
six are the *entire* "agency and systemic influence" section, which means the
positioning note currently argues the SIMEA contribution (§4) without engaging
a single one of the works that would situate it. If the influence-and-agency
literature is load-bearing for Proposition 1, §4 has to cite it; if it is not,
the reading list is claiming a foundation the argument does not use.

Arthur et al. (1997) is the most pointed absence: open decision 3 in §8 proposes
an agent-based market with a tunable autonomy parameter, and the Santa Fe
artificial stock market is the obvious precedent for exactly that design. It
should be discussed where the simulation is proposed, not filed in a reading
list.

**Aaronson is being asked to do two jobs.** §2.4 cites it both for the
self-prediction argument and as the reason to avoid leaning on quantum
indeterminacy. Those are different claims from different parts of a
hundred-page chapter, and neither is currently pinned to a section. Page-level
citations are needed before drafting.
